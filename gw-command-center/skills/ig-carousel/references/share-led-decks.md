# Share-Led Carousels

Roadmap idea 9. Installed in plugin 0.23.0 (2026-09-09). Editorial reference for share-led opted-in decks only: loaded by Forge after the brief and before copy for share-led variants, and by the carousel skill and batch for new share-led builds and explicit action rewrites to share. Adds no schedule, dependency, schema field, role, or archetype. Style-only rebuilds and legacy decks never apply it.

## The Job

Save means: I need this when I do the job later.
Share means: you and I need this to make a decision together.

Changing only the last slide from save to share is not a share-led rewrite. The body must explain the shared problem, acknowledge the recipient's perspective, and give both people a concrete next move.

## Automatic Brief

For new share-led content or an explicit rewrite to share, identify the sender, recipient, future moment, and shared decision. Draft one natural sentence the sender could write when forwarding the post. Keep that sentence in production notes, not as a fabricated endorsement.

Map these into existing brief fields: audience names sender/recipient; payoff names the joint decision; reason explains the moment and why another person is needed. Do not add schema fields.

Choose a source-supported approach:
- Missing information: each coach holds a reasonable piece of the plan; bring the pieces together.
- Shared constraints: reconcile assumptions about the room, equipment, staffing, or time.
- Shared language: help coaches explain an agreed purpose consistently.

These are rhetorical approaches, not new canonical archetypes. Keep the selected archetype and normal slide budget.

If no meaningful recipient or joint decision fits, repair the share angle within share first. If the source still cannot support it, the failure path depends on who chose share:

- Forge chose share automatically: choose the next feasible action from the brief policy and draft that. Record the rejected share angle and why in the brief's reason line. Nobody requested share, so this is not a silent switch.
- Scott requested share (an `action: share` reroll note or an explicit instruction): stop that draft for editorial repair. Leave the saved master and the polish note untouched, report the slug as REPAIR NEEDED naming the missing recipient or decision, and continue other topics. Do not invent a conflict, add a nighttime question, or mark the failed draft complete.

## Writing Rules

The cover names a shared situation, not a villain. The body makes the colleague's perspective understandable before identifying the gap. A useful body slide offers a question, comparison, or next action both people can use.

The CTA names recipient and purpose. The caption reinforces the same action. Do not stack save, share, comment, follow, and sales asks. Avoid humiliation, coach-bashing, or 'tag the coach who needs to hear this.' Do not promise that forwarding the post will resolve conflict.

## Five Pass/Fail Checks

1. Can we identify one plausible sender and recipient?
2. Would the recipient understand the situation without guessing the sender's intentions?
3. Does this help settle a real decision rather than merely provoke agreement?
4. Would a reasonable colleague receive it as help rather than a public correction?
5. Does a save-to-share reroll change the body, caption, and CTA together?

These are editorial checks, not a predicted share-rate score. No algorithm-ranking or revenue improvement is established.

## Compatibility

Apply only to newly opted-in share drafts and explicit action rewrites. Style-only changes preserve saved copy. Legacy decks and existing approved decks stay unchanged. Keep source quarantine, photo rules, voice gates, asset counts, saved-master rules, and approval tokens intact. Use ordinary body slides and existing copy fields.

No new role, schema, scheduler, publishing action, or permanent JSON master. A share-led post delivers its small useful answer freely. Verify current offers before any separate conversion follow-up; shares do not establish purchase intent.

### 1. The Whole Week

Sender: strength coach. Recipient: practice coordinator or head coach.
Moment: before adding work. Joint decision: who gathers the whole week's information?
Action: share. Archetype: System.
Source: C:/Claude Projects/Gridiron Warrior/wiki/concepts/weekly-load-ownership.md

#### Slide 1
Nobody has to make a bad call
for the week to get away from you.

#### Slide 2
The position coach wants better Indy work.
The head coach wants the team ready.
The strength coach has a plan, too.
Each sees a different part of the same athlete's week.

#### Slide 3
Before the next addition, put three things in the same conversation:
What is being added?
How long will it run?
Which athletes are getting it?
Include the JV kids also taking varsity reps.

#### Slide 4
The staff question:
Who brings the whole week into the room before we add to it?
That person needs the information.
It does not mean that person owns every decision.

#### Slide 5
Send this to the coach building your practice plan.
Put the field work and weight-room work in the same conversation.

#### Paired Caption
The missing piece may not be a better program. It may be getting everybody's piece of the week into the same room. Send this to the coach building practice and decide who will bring the whole picture.

Optional forwarding line: Can we look at our additions together before the next staff meeting?
Change from save: a personal checklist becomes an ownership decision with a named counterpart.
Boundary: no practice-script authority claim, external staff rating scale, injury-prevention promise, or invented safe-load threshold.

### 2. Write for the Same Room

Sender: head strength coach. Recipient: assistant helping write sessions.
Moment: before adapting another program. Joint decision: which assumptions must change for this school?
Action: share. Archetype: Teardown.
Source: C:/Claude Projects/Gridiron Warrior/wiki/concepts/resource-reality-hs-programs.md

#### Slide 1
Before you argue about exercises,
make sure you are programming for the same room.

#### Slide 2
One coach sees a good exercise list.
The other sees athletes waiting, a crowded rack, and the end of the period.
You can both be looking at a real problem.

#### Slide 3
ILLUSTRATIVE PLANNING EXAMPLE
30 athletes. 3 racks. One class period.
Sketch where each group goes and who coaches it before copying the session.
The exercise list does not answer those questions for you.

#### Slide 4
Sit down with the session and agree on:
The equipment available.
The groups training together.
Who supervises each station.
The time you actually have.
Then decide what the borrowed plan needs to lose or change.

#### Slide 5
Send this to the coach writing sessions with you.
Agree on the room before you debate the program.

#### Paired Caption
A borrowed program comes with somebody else's assumptions. Before you debate the exercise choices, agree on the people, equipment, and time in your building. Send this to the coach writing sessions with you.

Optional forwarding line: Let's sketch this in our room before we settle the exercise list.
Change from save: an individual audit becomes a shared design conversation that respects both coaches' concerns.
Boundary: illustrative numbers are not a supervision ratio, capacity standard, or guarantee of safe operation.

### 3. Give the Staff the Same Explanation

Sender: coach selecting camp accessory work. Recipient: assistant coaching that block.
Moment: before explaining exercise changes. Joint decision: how should the staff explain the purpose?
Action: share. Archetype: Formula.
Source: C:/Claude Projects/Gridiron Warrior/wiki/concepts/ego-proof-exercise-selection.md

#### Slide 1
The camp exercise changed.
Does the whole staff know why?

#### Slide 2
ILLUSTRATIVE STAFF SITUATION
An athlete sees a different lift.
One coach explains the purpose.
Another compares it with a summer number.
Now the athlete has two different ways to judge the session.

#### Slide 3
In Scott's camp approach, a suitable variation without an established PR can remove an unhelpful comparison.
Alternating dumbbell bench is one example from his material.
The point is the context, not changing exercises just to change them.

#### Slide 4
Before the group arrives, agree on three answers:
What job is this movement doing?
Why does it fit this block?
How will we explain today's work?
Give the staff a shared explanation, not just an updated exercise name.

#### Slide 5
Send this to the assistant coaching your camp accessory block.
Agree on the explanation before the athletes hear it.

#### Paired Caption
Changing the exercise is one decision. Getting the staff to explain the choice consistently is another. Send this to the assistant coaching your camp accessory block and settle the explanation together.

Optional forwarding line: Here's why I changed this block. Can we give the group the same explanation?
Change from save: an exercise-selection card becomes a communication task between coaches.
Boundary: staff dialogue is illustrative, not Scott's reported experience. No rehabilitation advice, load prescription, guaranteed motivation effect, or claim that novelty is always preferable.
